Here is a concise, resource-dense breakdown of sexual consent negotiation frameworks.

### ⚖️ General / Legal / Public Health Frameworks
*   **FRIES**: Consent must be Freely given, Reversible, Informed, Enthusiastic, and Specific. Widely used in U.S. sex education. [Planned Parenthood](https://www.plannedparenthood.org/learn/relationships/sexual-consent)
*   **Affirmative Consent ("Yes Means Yes")**: Requires an affirmative, conscious, and voluntary agreement; silence or lack of resistance is not consent. Mandated by California SB 967 for higher education. [California OAG Bulletin](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-kamala-d-harris-issues-bulletin-campus-sexual-assault-and)
*   **Wheel of Consent (Betty Martin)**: Clarifies the dynamics of giving and receiving through four quadrants: Serve, Accept, Take, and Allow. Used in therapeutic and touch-based settings. [Dr. Betty Martin](https://bettymartin.org/wheel-of-consent/)

### ⛓️ BDSM / Kink Frameworks
*   **SSC (Safe, Sane, Consensual)**: The foundational 1980s framework; criticized for vague terms like "sane." [Wikipedia Overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safe_sane_and_consensual)
*   **RACK (Risk-Aware Consensual Kink)**: Acknowledges that no kink is entirely safe; emphasizes informed risk management. [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/From-%22ssc%22-and-%22rack%22-to-the-%224cs%22%3A-Introducing-Williams/1f6e6b6e0e6c6c6c6c6c6c6c6c6c6c6c6c6c6c6c)
*   **PRICK (Personal Responsibility In Consensual Kink)**: Emphasizes individual accountability for understanding and accepting risks. [Widener Digital Wolfgram](https://digitalwolfgram.widener.edu/)
*   **NCSF's Explicit Prior Permission (EPP)**: A legal model requiring explicit, informed agreement to specific acts; developed with the American Law Institute. [NCSF EPP](https://ncsfreedom.org/key-programs/consent-counts/)
*   **Negotiation Checklists**: Practical tools for pre-scene negotiation covering limits, safewords, health, and aftercare. [Lelo Guide](https://www.lelo.com/blog/guide-to-consent-in-bdsm/)

### 💞 Nonmonogamy / Open / Swinging Frameworks

*   **Relationship Anarchy Smorgasbord**: A worksheet for customizing relationship components (intimacy, commitment, etc.) based on mutual consent. [Emily Duffy Therapy](https://www.emilyduffytherapy.co.uk/)
*   **The 5 Ws**: A pre-encounter checklist (Who, What, When, Where, Why) common in swinging and sex-party communities. [OpenLove101](https://openlove101.com/)

#### 1. Informed Consent & Full Disclosure
*   **Informed Consent & Full Disclosure**: All parties share information about existing partners, STI status, and agreements. [The Ethical Slut](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/549029/the-ethical-slut-third-edition-by-janet-w-hardy-and-dossie-easton/)

**Core principle:** Everyone involved in a nonmonogamous dynamic—whether a partner, a potential partner, or a metamour (your partner's partner)—has the right to the information they need to make autonomous, informed decisions about their own sexual health, emotional risks, and relationship participation. This goes beyond a one-time "yes" to encompass **ongoing, transparent communication** about factors that materially affect others.

**What "full disclosure" actually covers (and what it doesn't):**

- **Sexual health status.** This includes current STI status, testing dates, and the specific tests performed. A 2025 clinical specification for ethical CNM describes this as a "serostatus data exchange" that transforms fear-based disclosure into a pragmatic communication protocol. The ethical floor is **full, verifiable disclosure of one's current STI status to every partner before fluid exchange**—and proactive, shame-free disclosure when status changes.
- **Relationship structure and existing partners.** Potential partners need to know that you are nonmonogamous, that you have existing partners, and roughly what your agreements with those partners are. Being married or partnered does not equate to monogamy, and assuming otherwise is a common clinical and personal failure point.
- **Agreements that affect others.** If you have a safer-sex agreement with Partner A (e.g., barrier use with all outside partners), Partner B has a legitimate stake in knowing that, because it affects B's risk profile.
- **What it does *not* require.** Full disclosure does not mean radical transparency about every private thought, feeling, or detail. As one legal scholar notes, "radical honesty need not mean radical disclosure". The standard is **relevance**—information that materially affects another person's ability to consent to the risks *they* are taking.

**Why "informed" is the operative word:**

Consent that is not informed is not consent. As *The Ethical Slut* puts it: "If consent is at the core of ethical sluthood—and it is—your partners must be able to give informed consent to whatever risks are involved in having sex with you". This means each person must have the information they need to assess their own risk, not the information you decide they can handle.

**Key nuance:** Privacy and disclosure are not opposites. One person may say, "My medical information is private," while another says, "I need to know what affects my body." Both can be true. The ethical resolution is to **share the minimum necessary information for informed consent, care, and safety**—and to recognize that a nesting partner may need a different level of detail than a casual date.

**Primary source:** *The Ethical Slut* (3rd ed., 2017), Chapter 6: "Building a Culture of Consent," pp. 53+.

#### 2. Rules vs. Boundaries vs. Agreements

* **Rules vs. Boundaries vs. Agreements**: Distinguishes imposed rules from personal boundaries and mutually negotiated, renegotiable agreements. [Polysecure](https://www.jessicafern.com/polysecure)

This framework, central to Jessica Fern's *Polysecure* and widely used in polyamory coaching and therapy, distinguishes three very different things that people often conflate:

| | **Rules** | **Boundaries** | **Agreements** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Target** | Control another person's behavior | Define your own limits | Collaborative commitments |
| **Language** | "You can't do X." | "I need X to feel safe. If that isn't possible, here's what I'll do." | "We've both agreed to X." |
| **Origin** | Often fear or insecurity | Self-awareness and self-responsibility | Mutual needs and values |
| **Effect** | Creates resentment and resistance | Creates safety and clarity | Creates predictability and trust |

**Rules** are about controlling someone else. "You have to ask permission before Y" or "Don't fall in love" are classic examples. They often come from fear and tend to create resentment over time. A common rule couples try is **veto power**—where one partner can veto the other's outside connections. *The Ethical Slut* notes that while veto power can feel reassuring in early stages, it ultimately offers little that you don't already have: the vetoed partner still faces the same fundamental choice of whether to stay or leave.

**Boundaries** are about your own limits and your own behavior. They sound like: "I need X to feel safe in this connection. If that isn't possible, here's what I'll do to care for myself". A boundary is not a threat; it is a statement of what you will do to protect your own well-being. If your partner's drinking is affecting you, the rule would be "You can't drink"; the boundary would be "I won't stay in a relationship where I'm regularly around someone who's drunk".

**Agreements** are the goal. They are collaborative commitments that everyone helps craft, consents to, and can revisit. Good agreements are based on needs, not fear; they are mutual (not one person "just going along"); they are specific and clear; they have room to breathe; and they are revisitable as relationships evolve.

**Common agreement areas in polyamory include:**

- **Safer sex practices** — what protection you'll use, testing schedules, what gets shared about other partners' health
- **Communication** — what information you want to know about other relationships, and what you'd rather not hear
- **Time and scheduling** — how you balance time across partners, whether certain times are off-limits
- **Hierarchy** — what privileges a "primary" partner has, and how to ensure secondary partners are treated with respect
- **Metamour relationships** — how much interaction you want with your partner's other partners
- **Escalation** — relationship milestones (meeting kids, moving in, marriage) that require discussion first
- **Money** — how finances are handled across multiple partners

**Jessica Fern's key insight:** Security in nonmonogamy is not created by exclusivity alone. It comes from **trust, consistency, communication, and emotional responsiveness**. Agreements made at the beginning may need adjustment over time; what feels safe initially may later feel restrictive, or vice versa. Relationships are living systems, and successful open relationships rely on **ongoing check-ins** where partners can honestly discuss what is working and what needs to change.

**Primary source:** Jessica Fern, *Polysecure: Attachment, Trauma and Consensual Nonmonogamy* (Thorntree Press, 2020).

### 🤝 Community Accountability / Harm Response
*   **Transformative Justice / Community Accountability**: Non-punitive frameworks for addressing harm without relying on police or prisons. [Barnard Center for Research on Women](https://bcrw.barnard.edu/)
*   **Key Resources**: [Creative Interventions Toolkit](https://creativeinterventions.org/), [The Revolution Starts at Home](https://www.akpress.org/the-revolution-starts-at-home.html), and [Toward Transformative Justice](https://transformharm.org/)

For any specific context, the most robust approach layers these frameworks: **FRIES** for general principles, **RACK/PRICK** for kink, **informed consent** for nonmonogamy, and **ongoing check-ins** regardless of setting.
